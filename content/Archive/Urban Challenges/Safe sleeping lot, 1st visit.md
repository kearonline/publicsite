*The following is an excerpt from field notes produced during a visit to a safe sleeping lot in the City of San Diego, as part of the Urban Challenges in Homelessness course sequence. This visit involved conducting interviews with unhoused residents of the lot.*

James, Gilman, and I sat just outside the lot at the makeshift waiting area for the shuttle, a conglomeration of disparate yet cozy chairs and tables. It was our second of the day, and I had scarcely gotten used to the constant noise generated by the planes making their final approach into the nearby airport every five minutes or so. With it being our first time at the lot, we couldn’t fathom how people were able to live with such disruptions.

Our interview was wide-ranging and comprehensive, discussing everything from James’s background being incarcerated and living on the streets to what everyday life was like at the lot, but the topic always circled back to his hopes for the future. It was inspiring, to say the least, to hear about what he believed was his purpose and the approach to life that kept him going. Resilience has always been a major takeaway I have had after each conversation I have had with someone who is unhoused, but for some reason, this time, it felt different. Perhaps it was his gratitude for where he is now after going through so much, or the smaller details he mentioned like feeling like he is back on track to meet his personal goals after receiving medication treatment for mental illness, an experience that resonates deeply with my own.

Both Gilman and I were extremely nervous to get started. We discussed prior in the car ride going to the lot how I especially felt worried about saying ‘the wrong thing’ and how he was concerned about how the language barrier would impact our interview, but I was instantly put at ease as we started given how much grace and patience James showed towards us as well as how our prior interview went. By that time, those fears were dispelled and we both felt confident in how we did; I was especially happy to see Gilman taking the lead in the conversation given his initial fears. Our experience, though a little shaky at first, made us feel more confident about the interviews that were to come; having us treat this as more of a real conversation.

Our first visit to the lot was striking, even seeing it visually for the first time as I was driving to it given its appearance like an emergency shelter or even like a prison campsite. What stood out to me especially was the visual manifestation of rules and policies at the lot; from the single point of entry with a system that involves check-ins to the orderly layout of the tents with markings on the ground, it was clear that there are a lot of rules that people are held to account for. I’m not entirely sure how to describe how I felt seeing that, other than the fact that it did make me feel somewhat uncomfortable to see something that resembled carceral systems, but seeing this did inform my initial approach to the interviews and asking about the lot.
