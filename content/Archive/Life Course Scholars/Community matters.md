The past few weeks in the Life Course Scholars program have been nothing short of inspiring, saddening, and revelatory; sometimes all at once. It all opened my eyes to the depth and the variety of experiences shared by the older adults, a source of motivation in that by comparison, my life has only begun. It highlighted a sobering reality by showing certain extremes in the living conditions of older adults, a natural byproduct of income inequality that manifests itself in how comfortable people are allowed to live in old age. All in all, these experiences further reinforced that the aging experience is incredibly diverse and that, contrary to prior expectations, this kind of intergenerational dialogue is welcomed by all, if not most, and needed more than ever.

​Two communities that we had taken a look at throughout this time, Barrio Logan and La Jolla, could not be more different from a fundamental demographic level. Decades ago, the former was bisected by the expansion of a major freeway in the region, separating the community of people of color and lower-income residents and introducing a wide array of environmental and community health issues that continue to this day. The latter, a beach community, is one of the wealthiest in the region and in the nation.

It all brought to mind a prior reading discussion on a CityLife article by Mimi Kirk the existence of age-friendly cities and the kinds of amenities that are typically available within them, and which demographic groups are more likely to be able to reside in these communities. Barrio Logan has an especially vibrant sense of culture and community, the kind of busy street and civic life that can bring about strong connections between residents. However, we found that in a lot of areas, especially related to the infrastructure provided for pedestrians, older adults, and just people in general, that the built environment was not entirely conducive to community health. Fractured sidewalks, unmaintained public restrooms, and the lack of green space beyond Chicano Park all indicate continued underinvestment in this neighborhood. This lies in stark contrast to La Jolla, the location of our next visit to the beachfront Casa de Manana development. It is clear that the area is well-maintained and contains a lot of infrastructure dedicated to improving the quality of life of the older adults that live in it. The contrast and the fact that both are age-friendly in different ways supports both of Kirk’s findings in that age-friendly infrastructure is more of a consideration in wealthier and whiter communities, and that civic engagement remains a key component of age-friendliness.

It is one thing to read a theory, but it is a completely different experience seeing it manifest itself in real life as I saw in both communities. That each of these places find meaningful ways to engage its older adults, using whatever resources they have, is a source of hope for me. It was clear that older adults Barrio Logan are given many opportunities to engage with the arts through open galleries and studios, with the neighborhood’s long history of activism through dedicated spaces, and everything in between. At Casa de Manana, each resident we had spoken to was deeply involved in some way in an organization they had established at the facility, formed around anything from hobbies to religion to politics. At the West Center and the affordable housing developments we visited, like the others, there was a strong sense of community and shared purpose and identity that can be attributed to the work put in by every individual to foster it. It struck me as unique and particularly heartwarming the welcoming effect each community had, and how it led even the most introverted and individualist of people to want to engage with their neighbors.

I have seen this continued sense of community, not only between residents, but their families, friends, companions, and beyond first-hand in the interviews and further interactions I had with older adults along the way, some at the West Center Journey of Life event. One talked about their experience with homelessness and how Serving Seniors not only provided them with a space to live, but also people to rely on, a community to be a part of, and a place where they can continue to live a good life simply by “being good.” Another talked about their family and the similarities in the medical education path that they saw between their daughter, who goes to a local community college, and my co-interviewer. That I was able to have so many of these conversations, learn from them, and find places of connection with people who have very different life experiences from me is a true testament to the power of going into intergenerational dialogue with an open mind, as has been reinforced by so many throughout the course.

It was even more striking when our conversations veered into topics that normally would be considered taboo, like when one resident at Casa de Manana acknowledged the inequality that is highlighted when conditions between that facility and others are compared, and the role that she can play in helping to address it in small ways, like by volunteering her time at a local soup kitchen. When these conversations were able to be held as equals, contrary to all of the expectations I had going into the course, it only became more meaningful to me that I am able to be a part of it. That I was able to, with the class, put on the Journey Through Life event at the West Center and provide a space for them to provide advice or reflect on their life experiences at the ‘memory’ station.   
From trying to make the most out of each day to the saying that ‘good begets good’, there were so many exchanges of knowledge and of wisdom at this event that made me both emotional about the interactions I had, in part because of the optimism that so many carried about the future and the role we will play in it. It was especially nice to see the people attending the event returning to art and painting for example, hobbies that some hadn’t had the chance to do in so long, or talk about things they don’t usually talk about with undivided attention. I’m proud that as they expressed that, we, too, were able to provide something for them in the opportunity to look back and forward in their lives and to learn from us about our experiences in the world we are beginning to inherit from them. As I look ahead to the final presentation of my Oral History Project and my Healthy Aging Project, I look forward to carrying these experiences with me and to applying the many lessons I have learned from everyone of all ages into my daily life and beyond.
