It’s been a little over a month since our retreat, my first engagement as a Life Course Scholar program in the 2025-2026 cohort, and since then, there hasn’t been a dull moment. Our first outside ‘field trip’ to the Gary and Mary West Senior Wellness Center earlier this month showed me just a taste of how enriching this experience would come to be and continue to be. Beyond marking a surprising personal milestone as the first time I’d ever driven on any freeway as a result of peer pressure (I’m partially joking), it also proved to be one of great significance to me due to the nature of the single conversation I had with one of their regulars while serving lunch.

From long-winded discussions about politics and current events, to recollections about his upbringing in Texas, to relationship advice he’s learned over the years — per what he told me, I’m definitely going to try to learn how to dance — a lot of what this man, who is around the same age as my dad, had told me during our nearly hour-long discussion has stuck with me since then. Like many of those who I’d talked to after him, he too shared the growing sentiment that our generation would be the one to fix the societal problems that his generation had created, not taken seriously, or let worse (his words, not mine). Our generation would be the one with the fire and passion and the propensity to vote and participate in civic life needed to begin a course correction, and that, to him, was a source of hope.

I participated in incredibly meaningful and empowering discussions with several seniors who remained highly active in community and activism spaces during our affordable housing tour last week. Many of them had gone to the No Kings protest throughout the region the day before, and had recounted to us the importance of showing up for their communities and neighbors in times when they are under attack by the forces that be.   
To them, this was especially important having seen progress and decline in areas like civil rights for marginalized groups like people of color and the LGBTQ+. One talked about how she had helped bring spousal benefits to same-sex partners of employees when she was a part of a startup in the 1990s, when such a policy was virtually unheard of. To have seen society’s tolerance and acceptance of people like her waver and fluctuate over the years, as she mentioned, is truly surreal.   
It opened my eyes to the fact that many of the seniors I encountered were truly trailblazers at the height of their adulthood, like another person who had been an early adopter of computer animation and graphic design, or another who continues to adapt the concept of physical art snail mail to the modern, digital-dominant era. They laid the groundwork for future generations to have made remarkable progress in the areas in which they first pushed the boundaries.

There is no question that this is truly why the current state of the world, and how different it appears now, is on the minds of many that I have talked to. One step forward and two steps back and vice versa — many have seen the push and pull of progress, have seen what it means to be free (according to their own interpretation of that) and what it means for society to regress. Many talked about the freedom they had or used to have to travel without restriction and how they saw the plight of immigrants’ journeys (sometimes their own), recalling to mind how my own dad’s story, which I hope to tell for my Oral History Project, would perhaps scarcely be possible in today’s America.

​While occasionally heartbreaking, these conversations even moreso filled me with hope; that for all that the seniors I talked to have endured, they have always recognized the possibility of what comes next. They, too, have been in similar shoes feeling powerless and recognizing that power and energy from both people and movements are constantly in flux. They ‘waited their turn’ in their time and recognize that it will soon be ours to make the change we want to see in the world, and we are all the better for the wisdom that they bring us.
